SOS: Safra's budgeting system

Safra · 2020

Budgeting started in each department. The rework landed with planning.

At Safra, budget planning covered very different kinds of spending: office supplies, travel, sponsorships and philanthropy. Each department organized its numbers in Excel. Around thirty budget owners sent their spreadsheets to the planning team, which had to validate, consolidate and reconcile the whole set.

When something was wrong, the file went back. A correction arrived later, needed another check and re-entered consolidation. The team at the centre of the process often discovered problems when the spreadsheets reached them.

SOS, also known as Click Orçamentário, grew out of that situation. I led development and worked on the backend and database model. The story starts with the conversations that happened before the first screen.

We started by understanding the work

We spoke with the departments to understand how they prepared their budgets and how planning received and processed the files. We developed the solution with the people who would use it. Business teams brought their knowledge of the process; we helped explore what a shared application could change.

A distributed process needed to be accessible to budget owners across departments. Planning also needed visibility of progress without waiting for a file. Filling in a budget, following progress, requesting corrections, setting deadlines and approving figures became parts of the same discussion.

This opened the possibility of more proactive follow-up: spotting an issue during the cycle and requesting a change before closing. Alerts and visibility during preparation were among the possibilities we proposed. I describe them as proposals here; the exact features delivered and their behaviour still need confirmation.

The difficult part was giving the solution a shape

As I remember it, one of the initial ideas was to put the same spreadsheets on the internet. The business knew the process could improve but did not yet have a clear picture of what we could build. Agreeing on the solution was one of the early difficulties.

We had to spend time designing the solution and explaining user flows. Who would fill in the budget? What would planning be able to follow? How would a correction return to its owner? These drawings made the proposal understandable to people who knew the work but did not necessarily know what an application could make possible.

It was after that work of explanation and shared understanding that we convinced the departments. We shaped the solution together. That alignment belongs in the account of delivery alongside the screens and code.

Two conversations a week kept development close to the business

We met with the business team twice a week to align requests and set priorities. That rhythm kept development connected to the people preparing and consolidating the budget.

We also presented the proposal to directors and senior management. I spoke directly with the business-area superintendents to explain the process we were proposing. The discussion had to show how the change would help departments work together as well as introduce a new system.

I coordinated a team of four developers and shared backend and database implementation with another developer. Frontend, database, business and infrastructure teams had their own responsibilities. Connecting those areas was part of my work: a decision about data entry had to make sense in the interface, the server rules and the approval process.

Choosing a stack the bank could maintain

Angular and Node.js belonged to a set of technologies familiar to the bank and its developers. That familiarity influenced the choice. The system would need ongoing maintenance by people who knew that environment.

On the backend, we used TypeORM to work with the relational database from application code. Angular called the API; the server applied rules and persisted data. Each part had a responsibility along that path.

The interface could guide data entry. When the request reached the server, the rule still had to hold: an invalid forecast needed to be rejected there too.

The API separates editing a forecast from deciding its next step

These are two actual routes from the reconstruction. One edits the forecast; the other requests a state transition. Both call the same rule service. The HTTP handler stays short, and validation does not depend on which interface button was used. The service returns 422 for invalid input and 409 for an incompatible state or revision.

app.patch("/api/budgets/:id", async (req, res) =>
  res.json(await changeBudget(db, req.params.id, req.body, "edit")),
);
app.post("/api/budgets/:id/actions", async (req, res) =>
  res.json(await changeBudget(db, req.params.id, req.body, req.body?.action)),
);

Small parts, consistent screens

The professional responsible for frontend design introduced a compositional approach that caught my attention. Rather than treating each screen in isolation, the interface could be assembled from reusable parts. This is close to Atomic Design: small elements combine into larger groups until they form a page.

My focus was coordination and backend development. Colleagues contributed frontend design and implementation. The system needed to bring those contributions together into an experience that made sense to budget owners.

The current reconstruction makes the composition explicit. Buttons and status indicators are atoms; filters and the modal frame are molecules; the table and editor are organisms; the page coordinates API interactions. This organization is a reconstruction decision inspired by my recollection of the project, rather than a copy of its original component tree.

A small part reused across screens

This atom from the current reconstruction receives a status and renders it. The table does not need to repeat the markup for every indicator.

import { Component, Input } from "@angular/core";
import { labels, Status } from "../models";
@Component({
  selector: "sos-status",
  standalone: true,
  template: `<span class="status" [attr.data-status]="value">{{
    labels[value]
  }}</span>`,
})
export class StatusComponent {
  @Input() value: Status = "draft";
  labels = labels;
}

In the table organism, those same atoms are composed with row data and approval events. This excerpt comes from the current template; production authorization is not implemented in this demo.

One forecast, from entry to approval

To demonstrate the engineering, I rebuilt one slice of the process. A budget owner opens a forecast, changes the amount and explains the need. Angular validates the form. On save, the API repeats validation, writes the budget and records the change in the local database.

After submission, planning can approve or request a correction with a message. The owner corrects and resubmits it. An approved forecast can be forwarded to the fictional finance team. Its history shows those steps rather than only the final state.

The demonstration stores amounts in integer cents and uses a revision number to prevent an old edit from overwriting a newer one. The budget update and its event are saved in the same transaction. These are concrete decisions in the reconstruction; I am not claiming that every mechanism existed in this form in the original SOS.

TypeORM persists records in a local SQLite file through sql.js. This keeps the demo easy to run without connecting to any Safra database. Roles are simulated, data is fictional and history serves the demonstration; it is not a production audit trail.

The change and its history must be saved together

Excerpt from the reconstruction service, inside its TypeORM transaction. The revision condition detects stale edits; a failure aborts the transaction. The write queue used for the single sql.js connection is outside this excerpt. This is a current reconstruction decision, not recovered corporate code.

const revision = budget.revision + 1,
  at = new Date().toISOString();

What the reconstruction makes visible

The first demonstration reproduced the appearance and made the product recognizable. This version adds the path of information: a component collects input, a contract carries it to the server, a rule determines whether the change is allowed, and the database preserves the result.

There is also a limit to what this demonstrates. Seeing the stack run today does not itself establish how I managed the team in 2020. That part belongs in the account of responsibilities, business conversations and decisions made together.

The original system was adopted for budgeting across the bank. That is the outcome I report. I do not have an independent measurement of hours saved or errors reduced here, so I leave those figures out.

The difficulty of aligning on the solution explains some of the work that came before implementation. Drawing the flows and discussing what each person could do in the system were part of reaching an accepted proposal. That relationship between process, people and engineering is what I want to make visible when revisiting SOS.
